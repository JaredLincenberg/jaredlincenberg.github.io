Today I went to a BioBlitz in the Big Gardens at [Washington Park, Denver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Park,_Denver). I remember parking on the street and looking at my phone: 8:24 AM. I was early and excited. I then went to squeeze out the last bit of sunscreen from the tube and slather it all over, remembering to get my ears and forehead. I then saw it — magically it was 8:28 AM — and needed to walk quickly to arrive on time.

This was put together by [Bob Walker](https://www.inaturalist.org/people/bobw75) with Bonnie Sutherland from [Fans of Washington Park](https://www.fanswashingtonpark.org/park-projects), a sponsor of the [pollinator corridor](https://www.fanswashingtonpark.org/pollinator-corridor). We previously did a BioBlitz in the Mount Vernon Garden, a one-acre replica of Martha Washington's garden near Grasmere Lake; this time we were in the Big Gardens, three acres on the park's west side. For most of the last century these beds were displays of annuals, replanted every year. The last 5 years have been a steady push the other way — Skyler Smith, the Big Garden manager, pulling turf out and establishing native plant communities, creating a haven for pollinators. So we were standing in a place that is itself being made more room for other life. Thirty of us circled up at an intersection of paths, native plants in bloom all around — a feast for the eyes, with zips of motion throughout.

Bob started off the introduction and leaned on me for my knowledge of iNaturalist, clarifying the purpose of citizen science: to engage people with the world, and to alert scientists and conservationists about trends and unique discoveries. The BioBlitz is a snapshot of an area at a point in time, but also a touchpoint to bring people together and connect with the land. So the experience gives them a richer picture of the places they live or visit. To include all people, as nature includes all life.

There was also an employee of the Denver Parks and 2 ambassadors from [Xerces](https://xerces.org/) (April LeBaron and Sherry Gillespie), an organization dedicated to learning about and conserving nature. We had people who came from garden clubs, and a few kids and their parents from the nearby preschool and elementary school. All here to explore and learn. We were just finishing introducing ourselves to everyone when Julie Morris, a teaching professor at DU, came with some useful equipment: nets, scissor bug catchers, magnifiers, containers, and a cooler. I tend to go out purely armed with my hat and sunglasses and my phone, to immerse myself in the path or trail I choose to follow, enjoying the challenge of getting within fractions of an inch of insects and other arthropods. For this event it was about sharing the discoveries. Most of the items were straightforward, but a few had never seen a cooler used in the observation of insects. The cooler was used to chill the insect in the test tube in ice for 15 to 25 minutes depending on the size; this slows them down so we could photograph and observe in detail the wonder of the insects' exoskeleton. A few people were worried that it would hurt the insects, being captured and nearly frozen.

I feel that the collection and manipulation of creatures in this way should be done in furtherance of some good. For science and community education and engagement, I feel, are noble and do more good. Nature is something we are a part of and not something we simply observe, so that means using it — documenting, foraging, medicinal herbs, hiking, camping — it means respect, not separation. This is a problem about 'leave no trace': it treats nature as pristine, and us as only a hindrance. It ignores the damage already done: weedy takeover, monocultures, the annual beds these gardens used to be — showy, colorful, but transient, lacking what native plants provide to the ecosystem. Isolating nature as something you go see, not something that is always around and part of you, is the great loss of industrialization.

The process doesn't hurt the insect — they all warmed up and flew off within 5 to 10 minutes.

We were all present, eager, we spread out to see those things that were zipping out of the plants. I offered to help people new to iNaturalist learn how to use the app, going over taking pictures, uploading, and identifying with the Computer Vision AI — an amazing accomplishment by the team at iNaturalist, but one that needs to be understood. It guesses based on previous observations, so the quality varies on the rarity, the distinguishing features, and the framing of the images. It is a guide, not an oracle; a start, not an end; an insight, not a conclusion. I had to hold myself back from showing every little detail and trick I have uncovered, but kept it to the core functions so they could start observing with everyone else.

I had made a few observations and even collected a bee and a drone fly (a bee mimic). I then switched to helping Julie with the cooler and transferring the bugs to the test tubes. Once the first honey bee was chilled enough, we brought some people back and showed them the bugs and talked about what they were and what they do. We had honey, bumble, sweat and long-horned bees, beetles, and drone flies. This was a chance to engage the kids there, teaching them the diversity of pollinators. I was responsible for taking pictures and showing the different features; what helped was using my phone to magnify and get up close to the insect and show it clearly on the phone screen.

Time had flown by with most people getting ready to go. I helped clean up and bring the tools back to Julie's car. Acknowledging the high turn out and how Bob and Bonnie were able to reach out and bring these people together to be a part of nature.

Afterward I went back to the garden and was able to talk shop with Bob and his friend [David Martin](https://www.inaturalist.org/people/davidemartin). Talking about the difference between smartphones and digital cameras. Bragging about our best pics and rarest sightings. Reflecting on the desire to take more pictures documenting the life in Washington Park.

We then split, and I took some time to myself and continued documenting and exploring, in this haven for pollinators. Sometimes it was just watching the bees fly around and hoping to spot a little fly or a rolly polly, isopods. Other times it was chasing after butterflies, trying not to scare them. I even saw a dragonfly perching in the garden. I took my last picture at 12:32 PM. It was an amazing experience — being part of the group and helping lead, but also taking time to enjoy them at a slower pace.
